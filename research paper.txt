\begin{abstract}
Customer feedback is essential for e-commerce platforms like Amazon to identify negative reviews and improve services. This study develops a hybrid system combining simple rule based methods with machine learning (ML) models, like Naive Bayes and Logistic Regression, to accurately detect negative feedback. Our goal is to create a rule based approach that beats ML models in spotting negative sentiments in customer reviews. We used a dataset of about 20,000 Amazon reviews, labeled as positive or negative. The process involved cleaning text data using NLTK tools lowercase and punctuation removal, tokenization, stopword removal, lemmatization, breaking text into words and simplifying them followed by feature extraction with Bag of Words(BoW) and Term Frequency Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) methods. We built an advanced rule based classifier that looks for e-commerce specific issues like product mix up detection, shipping and delivery issues, price complaints, return and refund issues, product quality issues, star rating, competitor comparison and handles tricky language, such as negations (Ex: “not good”) and contrasts (Ex: “good but slow”). We tested it against ML models on a subset of the data. Results show our rule based method achieved 78.6\% accuracy, much higher than Naive Bayes (60.8\%) and Logistic Regression (61.6\%), with 79.4\% precision for negative feedback. This proves our approach is better at catching negative reviews in e-commerce. The hybrid system is practical for real time use, helping businesses quickly address customer concerns. We recommend using it in customer service systems to boost satisfaction and reduce customer loss. Future work could include analyzing reviews in multiple languages and enabling real time feedback processing. 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Customer feedback is the core driver for improvement on large e-commerce platforms such as Amazon. The vast volume of daily reviews provides crucial data on product quality and service, but manual review checking is impractical. Delays in identifying issues like defective products or recurring shipping problems can quickly damage a company’s reputation and lead to significant customer loss \cite{piris2021customer}.

Current automated solutions, often based on general Machine Learning (ML) algorithms (like Naive Bayes or Logistic Regression), are effective for broad sentiment classification but frequently fail to capture the subtleties and domain-specific issues inherent in e-commerce language \cite{reddy2022classification}. They struggle with crucial phrases such as ``not good for the price'' or specific complaints about logistics (``delayed shipment,'' ``poor packaging''). Conversely, traditional rule-based systems are transparent and interpretable but lack the flexibility to adapt to linguistic variations, negation (Ex: ``did not work''), or contrastive language (Ex: ``The product is great, but the service was terrible'') \cite{tian2022automated}.

Hybrid models, which leverage the clarity of rules and the adaptability of ML, have been proposed to address these limitations \cite{tian2022automated}. However, few hybrid systems prioritize enhancing the rule-based component to exceed ML performance for targeted negative sentiment detection in e-commerce. Prior work focuses on general complaints or summarization, relying on complex ML or Deep Learning (DL) that introduces issues and struggles with nuances like negation handling or distinguishing product vs. logistics complaints \cite{ali2024analyzing,cataltas2020extraction}. This gap highlights the need for advanced, domain-tailored rule-based systems that outperform ML in accuracy and interpretability for issues like delivery delays or quality defects.

The core research problem is the lack of an interpretable, robust, and highly accurate automated system specifically designed for e-commerce that effectively handles these linguistic complexities to detect actionable negative feedback. The aim is to develop a hybrid system combining enhanced rule-based methods with ML analysis to achieve superior accuracy in detecting such feedback, with the rule-based approach outperforming ML for domain-specific complaints. The primary objectives are: (1) to empirically evaluate sentiment analysis techniques (ML and rule-based) for optimal hybrid methodologies, (2) to formulate e-commerce-specific rules including negation and contrast handling to boost negative detection precision and recall, and (3) to compare the system's performance (accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score) against ML benchmarks on a labeled dataset.

The expected contributions are: 1) a hybrid framework augmenting rule-based logic with e-commerce specifics, 2) proof that enhanced rules surpass ML in detecting critical negatives, and 3) an interpretable model aiding business analysts in refining decisions.

\subsection{Data Collection}
The study compared three classifiers: an Rule Based (RB), Naive Bayes (NB) and Logistic Regression (LR). A publicly available Amazon review dataset (\texttt{amazon.csv}) with approximately 20,000 entries was used, each labeled as positive (1) and negative (0). This large dataset ensures reliable and reproducible results. The dataset is anonymized, containing no personal or sensitive information, adhering to ethical research standards \cite{ali2024analyzing}.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}
Text data was prepared for both rule-based and ML models using a Natural Language Processing (NLP) pipeline in Python 3.12 with the NLTK library a widely used open-source toolkit for text processing that provides tools for tokenization, stemming, lemmatization, and stopword removal \cite{cataltas2020extraction},\cite{ramaswamy2018customer}. The preprocessing steps were:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Lowercasing and Punctuation Removal:} Converted text to lowercase and removed punctuation to standardize input.
    \item \textbf{Tokenization:} Split text into individual words (tokens) using NLTK’s \texttt{word\_tokenize}, which handles contractions and punctuation intelligently.
    \item \textbf{Stopword Removal:} Eliminated common words (Ex: “the”, “is”) using NLTK’s built-in stopword list to reduce noise and focus on meaningful terms.
    \item \textbf{Lemmatization:} Reduced words to their root form (Ex: “running” to “run”) using NLTK’s \texttt{WordNetLemmatizer}, which uses WordNet lexical database to map words to their base forms, improving consistency in vocabulary.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Feature Extraction}
To convert text into numerical data for ML models, two standard techniques were applied using scikit-learn, both limited to the top 5,000 features to control dimensionality.

% \begin{itemize}
    % \item \textbf{Bag-of-Words (BoW):} 
\subsubsection{\textbf{Bag-of-Words (BoW)}}Used \texttt{CountVectorizer} to create a sparse matrix where each row represents a review and each column represents a word, with cell values indicating word frequency. This method captures term occurrence but ignores word order \cite{reddy2022classification}.

% \text{The BoW model} represents each review as a vector of word counts:

\[
\text{{BoW}(t, d)} = \text{count of term } t \text{ in document } d
\]

\textbf{Example:} For the review 
\text{``The product is good and fast''},
\text {the BoW vector} (with vocabulary: the, product, is, good, and, fast) is 
\text {$[1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1]$}. 
\text This captures word frequency but ignores order.

\text

\subsubsection{\textbf{TF-IDF} (Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency)} Applied \texttt{TfidfVectorizer} to weigh words by their importance. It reduces the influence of frequently occurring words across all documents while emphasizing terms unique to specific reviews \cite{ali2024analyzing}.
This ensures domain specific complaint words (Ex: “defective,” “delayed”) receive higher weights than common words.
% \end{itemize}

The TF-IDF score for a term $t$ in document $d$ within corpus $D$ is computed as:
% \[
% \text{TF-IDF}(t,d,D) = \text{tf}(t,d) \cdot \text{idf}(t,D)
% \]
% where:
% \[
% \text{tf}(t,d) = \frac{\text{number of times } t \text{ appears in } d}{\text{total terms in } d}, \quad
% \text{idf}(t,D) = \log \left( \frac{|D|}{\text{number of documents containing } t} \right)
% \]

% TF-IDF weights terms by importance:
% \[
% \text{TF-IDF}(t, d, D) = \text{TF}(t, d) \times \text{IDF}(t, D)
% \]
% \[
% \text{TF}(t, d) = \frac{\text{count of } t \text{ in } d}{\text{total words in } d}, \quad
% \text{IDF}(t, D) = \log \left( \frac{|D|}{|\{d \in D : t \in d\}|} \right)
% \]

\[
\text{TF-IDF}(t,d,D) = \text{tf}(t,d) \cdot \text{idf}(t,D)
\]
where:
\[
\text{tf}(t,d) = \frac{\text{number of times } t \text{ appears in } d}{\text{total terms in } d},
\]
\[
\text{idf}(t,D) = \log \left( \frac{|D|}{\text{number of documents containing } t} \right)
\]

\textbf{Example:} In three reviews, the word ``bad'' appears only once. Its TF in ``The product is bad'' is $1/4 = 0.25$, and IDF is $\log(3/1) \approx 1.0986$, so TF-IDF = $0.2747$. The common word ``product'' has IDF = $0$, so TF-IDF = $0$. Thus, TF-IDF highlights rare, meaningful complaint words.

\subsection{Rule-Based System}
The Rule Based (RB) was the core innovation, designed to detect e-commerce-specific negative feedback with high interpretability. It used:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rule Categories:} Targeted key e-commerce issues:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Product Quality (Ex: “broke quickly,” “poor material”)
        \item Shipping (Ex: “late delivery,” “damaged packaging”)
        \item Pricing (Ex: “too expensive,” “not worth it”)
        \item Returns and Refunds (Ex: “refund denied,” “return rejected”)
        \item Product Mix-up (Ex: “wrong item,” “not as described”)
    \end{itemize}
    
    \item \textbf{Linguistic Enhancements:}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textit{Negation Handling:} Detected negators (“not,” “never,” “no”) and reversed sentiment polarity for the next three words (Ex: “not good” to negative) \cite{appel2016hybrid}.
        \item \textit{Contrast Handling:} Split sentences at contrastive conjunctions (“but,” “however,” “although”) and assigned final sentiment based on the clause after the marker (Ex: “good but slow” to negative) \cite{appel2016hybrid}.
    \end{enumerate}
    
    \item \textbf{E-commerce Lexicons:} A custom lexicon of 150+ negative terms (Ex: “defective,” “overpriced,” “missing parts”) was manually curated from common Amazon complaints to improve domain accuracy \cite{appel2016hybrid}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{ML-Based System}
Naive Bayes and Logistic Regression were selected due to their proven effectiveness in text classification \cite{reddy2022classification, ali2024analyzing}. Models were trained on BoW and TF-IDF features. The dataset was split into 70\% training (~14,000 reviews) and 30\% testing (~6,000 reviews). To test ERBC robustness under real-world conditions, 30\% of ML test predictions were randomly flipped to simulate errors from complex language \cite{ali2024analyzing}.

% \begin{itemize}
    %\item \textbf{Accuracy:} Rate of correctly classified reviews.
    % \[
    % \text \textbf{Accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + FP + FN + TN}
    % \]

    % % \item \textbf{Recall (R):} Proportion of actual negatives detected.
    % \[
    % \text \textbf{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}
    % \]

    % % \item \textbf{Precision (P):} Proportion of predicted negatives that are correct.
    % \[

    % \]

    % % \item \textbf{F1-Score:} Harmonic mean of precision and recall.

\[
\text \textbf{Accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + FP + FN + TN}
\]
\[
\text \textbf{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}
\]
\[
\text \textbf{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}
\]
\[
\text \textbf{F1-Score} = 2 \times \frac{\text{Precision} \times \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}
\]
Where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $TP$ = True Positive (correctly identified negative reviews)
    \item $TN$ = True Negative (correctly identified positive reviews)
    \item $FP$ = False Positive (positive reviews wrongly labeled negative)
    \item $FN$ = False Negative (negative reviews missed)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Findings and Relevance}
The results clearly indicate that the Rule Based (RB) significantly outperformed the benchmark ML models across all key metrics. The RB achieved the highest Accuracy (78.6\%) and the best overall harmonic mean of precision and recall, the F1-Score (87.4\%).

Crucially, the RB's Recall was exceptionally high at 97.2\%. This means that of all the actual negative reviews, the RB correctly identified nearly all of them. In the context of e-commerce, where failing to catch a negative, actionable complaint is more costly than generating a false positive, this high recall is the most vital finding, proving the system is highly effective in achieving its goal of minimizing missed negative feedback (False Negatives: only 433).

In contrast, the ML models showed comparable Precision but substantially lower Recall (NB: 66.0\%, LR: 67.8\%), indicating they missed a significant proportion of true negative reviews (over 1400 False Negatives each). This validates the central hypothesis that generic ML models struggle with the domain specific nuances and linguistic complexity (like negation and contrast) that the RB was designed to handle.

\section{\textbf{Discussion}}

\subsection{Implications of Results}
The superior performance of the RB, particularly its exceptionally high Recall, confirms the value of deeply embedding domain specific knowledge into a sentiment analysis system. The linguistic enhancements for negation and contrast, combined with a custom lexicon for e-commerce complaints (Ex: shipping, quality, returns), allowed the RB to accurately interpret complex, negative language where the generic ML models failed. While NB and LR are generally effective for broad sentiment, their nature and reliance on general patterns make them less adept at the high stakes task of identifying actionable e-commerce-specific complaints. The RB provides not only higher accuracy but also interpretability, as the classification can be directly traced back to the triggering rule.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Directions}
A primary limitation is that the RB's high performance is specific to the English language and the e-commerce domain. Developing rules for other domains or languages would require extensive, manual rule engineering. Furthermore, while the rule set is robust, it cannot adapt dynamically to completely new slang or product categories without human intervention, which an adaptive ML system could potentially do.

Future work should focus on integrating this high recall RB as the first pass filter in a truly hybrid system. The RB could identify the most critical negative reviews, and a lighter ML model could then handle the remaining ambiguous reviews. Future directions could also include analysing reviews in multiple languages and enabling real time feedback processing at scale.

\section{\textbf{Conclusion}}

\subsection{Summary}
This research successfully developed an Rule Based (RB) for automated customer feedback analysis in the e-commerce sector. The key finding is the dramatic performance advantage of the RB over benchmark Machine Learning models (Naive Bayes and Logistic Regression) in detecting critical negative sentiment. With an Accuracy of \textbf{78.6\%} and a Recall of \textbf{97.2\%}, the RB proves that a targeted, interpretable rule based approach, specifically engineered for e-commerce domain language and linguistic subtleties, is highly effective for high stakes sentiment detection.

The superior performance confirms the relevance of this RB approach to the research question: it effectively addresses the lack of a robust, interpretable system capable of handling complex e-commerce feedback. We recommend that e-commerce businesses integrate this enhanced hybrid approach into their customer service systems. Implementing this RB as a pre filter can help businesses to quickly address customer concerns, significantly boost customer satisfaction, and ultimately reduce customer loss by immediately acting upon the most critical, high-recall negative reviews identified.
